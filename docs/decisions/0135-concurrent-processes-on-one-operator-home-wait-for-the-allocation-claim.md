# ADR-0135: Concurrent processes on one operator home wait for the allocation claim

- **Status**: Accepted (the direction) — 2026-11-18. ## Context

The native adapter keeps a private, bounded ownership inventory under the operator's home (`.relavium-fs-private-v1/fs-staging-v1`). Allocating a reservation, cleaning up after a publication, cleaning up before a pin and a recovery pass each take an exclusive kernel claim on `inventory.lock`, **with one nonblocking attempt**: any failure, including "another process holds it", was returned as the `changed` refusal. The TypeScript host turns any native stage failure into a non-retryable `effect_needs_attention`. Every native stage also runs a recovery pass before it reserves, so recovery is on the write path.

Several processes on one home (two terminals, a terminal and an editor extension) are a supported way to work. The probes below ran on the built addon (macOS) with several writers **in one process, on separate descriptors**, which conflict under `flock` exactly as separate processes do. An independent reviewer then ran real OS processes on macOS with throwaway scripts (not kept in the repository) and got the same picture: with the claim forced back to one attempt, two processes with 30 writes each completed 30 of 60 and three processes 30 of 90 (the rest `changed` or `possibly_published:uncertain`); with the wait, 2, 3 and 6 processes completed every write, and twelve cold starts of six processes on a fresh home completed 72 of 72. Two runs of the first in-process probe agree: on 2026-11-17, 27 of 60 writes failed at stage and 3 ended uncertain; on 2026-11-18, 28 failed and 2 ended uncertain.

| Probe (before the change) | Result |
|---|---|
| Two concurrent writers, 30 writes each (2026-11-18) | 28 of 60 failed at stage with `changed:not_needed`; 2 more ended `possibly_published:uncertain` (the target was written, but the post-publication cleanup could not take the claim, so the outcome could not be settled); **30 of 60 completed cleanly** |
| 200 recovery passes while a lock-less churner created and removed `.reserved` entries (what a producer's pin does to its reservation) | 102 failed with `changed` (78 in the 2026-11-17 run); the same probe with no churn: 0 of 200 |

The second row is a separate defect: a scan lists an entry, then finds it gone, and calls the whole index changed. The TypeScript inventory reader, which every filesystem tool call runs, has the same windows (the entry gone before it is opened, unlinked after it was opened, or already at link count zero when it is looked at), so a harmless race could surface to the user as a refused call as well.

## Decision

1. **The allocation claim is waited for.** The nonblocking attempt is repeated about every two milliseconds while another handle holds the claim. Reserving and a recovery pass wait inside the operation's own two-second deadline and cancellation flag; a raised flag is `cancelled` and an exhausted deadline is the same `changed` refusal as before. **Cleanup after a publication and cleanup before a pin wait under a fresh two-second budget and ignore cancellation, on purpose, and beyond the waiter limit (decision 2) they get a longer grace than a reservation:** cleanup completion suppresses cancellation (ADR-0126), a cleanup cannot be retried, and a refused cleanup after a publication is an uncertain write. Each claim site has its own budget, so a write that runs a recovery pass, reserves and later cleans up can wait several times two seconds in the worst case. **Work done under a claim, at every one of these sites, measures its budget from the instant the claim was acquired**, not from before the wait, so a wait that used the deadline up cannot make a claim that was just obtained fail on its first step or leave a cleanup half done. After the wait the lock file is opened by name again and must still be the file that was locked: another process may have unlinked and recreated it, and a claim on the orphaned inode would split the exclusion domain.
2. **At most all but one worker-pool thread waits at once.** Every native call runs on Node's shared worker pool (four threads unless `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE` says otherwise, read the way libuv reads it), which also serves `fs`, `dns` and `crypto`; parking all of it behind a stuck holder (a process stopped inside its critical section, a slow network home) would stall every other async call in the CLI, provider connections included. So at most `pool size - 1` threads per process wait (three by default; a pool of one or two weakens the guarantee, because the limit is then zero or one). **A reservation or recovery beyond that limit is not refused at once: it polls for a short grace of 100 ms**, so a fourth concurrent caller behind a holder that is merely busy (a holder in another process is the supported case) still gets through, while a stuck holder costs the one remaining thread only that grace per caller. Past the grace it gets the old `changed` refusal; nothing was written yet. Cancellation and the deadline are checked before the limit, so a cancelled caller is `cancelled`, never `changed`. A cleanup counts toward the limit too, and beyond it gets a grace of 500 ms instead of 100 ms: it cannot be retried, so a refusal costs more than a refused reservation does, yet it must not park the pool for the full two seconds either (cleanups left exempt from the limit stalled unrelated async calls for 1.8 s when four were in flight behind a stuck holder).
3. **Only contention is repeated.** `EWOULDBLOCK`, `EAGAIN` and `EINTR` for `flock`, `ERROR_LOCK_VIOLATION` for `LockFile`. A bad descriptor, a full lock table or any other error fails at once: retrying cannot fix it and must not be mistaken for a holder that will go away.
4. **The per-record claim stays a single attempt.** A held record claim means a live producer owns that record (recovery retains it). A writer holds its own record claim while it waits for the allocation claim, and recovery holds the allocation claim while it tries a record claim, so waiting on both would make a wait cycle.
5. **A scan skips an uncommitted entry that vanishes, in the Rust scan and in the TypeScript reader.** A producer pins without any claim a reader holds: it renames `.prepare` to `.record` and removes `.reserved`, and a completing write removes its `.record`. A scan that meets an uncommitted entry gone before it opens it, or after (the file it holds is then unlinked: the same inode, but its link count reads zero), skips it; after a refusal the entry only counts as vanished when its name is really absent. The TypeScript reader has a third window the Rust scan does not: its `lstat` of an entry that is being unlinked can still succeed with a link count of zero (seen on macOS: 110 times in 5.2 million probes of one churner) while the name is, for a moment, still there, so for that case the reader does not require the name to be absent. In the TypeScript reader a committed `.record` that vanishes in any of those ways runs the whole scan again (up to eight whole scans inside the same deadline, because under heavy churn a record can vanish in several scans in a row), as one that vanished before the open already did; in the Rust scan a `.record` and the lock file are removed only by holders of the allocation claim, which the scan also holds, so their absence stays a changed index. A file that is still there and fails a check stays fatal. A skipped entry **still counts toward the 128-nonce bound and is listed** (a recovery pass reports it as retained, which a mid-promotion entry is): a directory listing can omit an entry renamed while it runs, so leaving it out let `reserve` allocate past the bound, after which every scan, recovery included, failed closed. `reserve` still refuses at 128; both scans tolerate eight more nonces, so that an overshoot caused by pins that were in flight is transient instead of failing closed with nothing supported to clear it.
6. **Two small hardenings in the same code.** `Record::encode` enforces every invariant `Record::decode` requires, through shared validators: a positive `i32` producer id, an owner and a locator within their bounds, a valid nonce, and an absolute locator **in normalized form** (written exactly as `Path::components` would write it back: no `..` component, no `.` in the middle, no repeated or trailing separator) whose last name is `<prefix><pid>.<nonce>`. A record that can be written is therefore accepted by the Rust decoder, and its locator by the TypeScript reader's rule (`resolve(stage) === stage`); the host passes realpath'd paths, so the rule is not reachable in practice, and an index from before this change needs no migration (the application is still in development). And a pin removes its `.reserved` only after re-proving that the file under the name is the one it created, as `cleanup` already does.

Considered: **a blocking lock call** with no polling (rejected: it cannot honour the deadline or the cancellation flag, it parks a worker thread without bound, and `flock`, `LockFileEx` and the Windows byte-range lock differ in what interrupts them); **retrying in the TypeScript host** on `changed:not_needed` (rejected: the host cannot tell contention from a real change, cannot retry a `possibly_published` cleanup because the producer has already handed the stage over, and a retry there touches the effect journal's semantics); **a distinct "busy" native result that the host retries without holding a thread** (not now: it is a host and effect-journal change, and the limit with its grace already keeps the pool free; it stays the option if the limit proves too tight); **serialising writers inside one process only** (rejected: the supported case is several processes); **a PID or lease file in front of the claim** (rejected: new stale-state failure modes where the kernel claim is already released on process death); **raising the nonblocking attempt count with no sleep** (rejected: it burns a core and still ends at an arbitrary count).

## Consequences

### Positive

- Concurrent writers queue instead of dropping. Measured on the rebuilt addon (macOS, in-process writers): two writers, 30 writes each, **60 of 60 completed cleanly (before: 30 of 60, plus 2 that wrote the target but ended uncertain)**; four writers 120 of 120; eight writers 240 of 240; two writers with a recovery loop running at the same time, 60 of 60 writes and 2,288 of 2,288 recoveries; the churn probe 200 of 200 (before: 98 of 200). No leftover inventory entries after any run. The real host write path (prepare, stage, publish, release through the built addon) is covered by a test that runs three concurrent writers and fails with `NativeWriteNeedsAttentionError` when the claim is one nonblocking attempt again (`fs-native-concurrency.test.ts`, POSIX only).
- Unrelated async work is not parked behind a stuck holder. With writers behind a claim held by another process, `fs.stat`, `dns.lookup` and `crypto.randomBytes` submitted 200 ms after the writers started took 0 ms with up to four writers, about 0.1 s with six and about 0.3 s with eight (an independent run measured 4, 107 and 313 ms): each caller beyond the limit polls for its 100 ms grace, one after another, on the one thread that is never parked, so the delay is about 0.1 s per caller beyond the limit. The first version of the wait, with no limit and no grace, stalled them 1.8 s with four writers and 3.8 s with eight.
- A record can no longer be written that a later scan cannot read, and the TypeScript reader no longer refuses a tool call because a producer finished pinning at the wrong moment.

### Negative

- **Not every collision is absorbed.** Behind a holder that does not let go, three writers per process wait two seconds and every further reservation is refused after the 100 ms grace; with three processes of four concurrent writers each, an independent run saw 6 of 240 writes refused at stage (`changed`) and, before cleanups were exempted from the limit, 1 more end uncertain. A refused reservation wrote nothing, but the host reports it as `effect_needs_attention`, so the host is not unaffected in these cases. Four concurrent native writes in one process need parallel workflow branches or several sessions: the tool calls of one agent turn run one at a time.
- A waiting writer occupies a worker thread for up to two seconds, and the claim is not fair (`flock` and `LockFile` give no ordering). A recovery pass holds the claim for a whole scan, up to its own deadline, and can make a writer miss its own budget; the refusal is the existing one. A cancelled write can spend up to two more seconds in its cleanup. A cleanup beyond the limit waits 500 ms behind a stuck holder, so unrelated async calls can be delayed by about that much per such cleanup, and a cleanup refused after that grace is an uncertain write.
- The evidence is partly indirect. The in-process probes behind the headline numbers (28 of 60, 98 of 200, the stall times) are kept as developer tools in `tools/native-fs/concurrency-probes/` (run against the pre-change binary they show 21 stage failures and 9 uncertain outcomes in 60 writes, and 48 of 100 failed recovery passes); the multi-process runs were a reviewer's throwaway scripts and are not kept; the measurements ran on macOS, and the numbers in this ADR come from separate runs that vary. Linux and Windows are verified only by the eight-target CI. The end-to-end test is skipped on Windows, because the required `windows-concurrency` job would otherwise run an unverified platform as a required check.
- Windows is unproven here beyond a type check. A Windows scan can meet an entry in a delete-pending state (an access-denied or sharing-violation error instead of "not found"), which this ADR does not claim to handle; the stress test and the other POSIX-only tests are excluded from the Windows population, each for its own POSIX-specific reason (link counts, replacing a held file, delete-pending). Network homes that emulate `flock` with byte-range locks (NFS, SMB) are unverified too.
- The wait widens the window in which another same-user process can replace the lock file; the identity check after the wait closes it for the claim just taken, and a same-user process could always do the rest. The retired-index check is made once per operation, so an old build that appears during a wait is not noticed (an unsupported mixed mode).
- **Pre-existing and not changed here: entries leaked by a crashed producer are never reclaimed.** Recovery retains every `.reserved` and `.prepare` and every `.record` whose stage it cannot prove, so repeated crashes (an independent run of 40 kills left 84 retained entries and recovery removed none) can fill the 128 slots, after which every native write is refused with nothing supported to clear it. A supported reclaim is a design decision for a later ADR; it is recorded in the register.
- Outside this change, as separate items in the register: a cold-start race when two processes create the index hierarchy on a fresh home at the same moment (twelve independent cold starts of six processes completed 72 of 72, which is evidence, not a proof), the Linux and Windows parity findings (a setgid parent directory, the link-count check on Windows, the Linux private-file parent check, the staged-payload re-proof), closing the producer's reservation handle after the pin removes it (a possible Windows hardening), and the exported `recoverMetadataStaging` entry, which no product code calls although every native stage runs a recovery pass.
