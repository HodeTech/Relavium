# ADR-0135: Concurrent processes on one operator home wait for the allocation claim

- **Status**: Accepted — 2026-11-18. The direction is the maintainer's: on 2026-11-17 they ruled that several `relavium` processes on one operator home are a supported mode, and on 2026-11-18 they ordered this fix before MVP-4. The mechanism below was designed and implemented by the assistant and has had two rounds of independent review (the second found no blocker or high; findings and dispositions are in the [register](../roadmap/deferred-tasks.md)). Still owed, and not claimed here: the maintainer's reading of this text, a final cumulative review round and a separate security sitting on the last delta, a probe with two real OS processes, and the eight-target CI.
- **Date**: 2026-11-18
- **Amends**: [ADR-0126](0126-native-write-admission-and-recovery-keep-explicit-lifetime-proof.md) (the allocation claim and the inventory scan)
- **Related**: [ADR-0125](0125-file-writes-preserve-security-metadata-through-a-native-host-adapter.md), [ADR-0127](0127-macos-write-metadata-has-distinct-parent-and-file-profiles.md), [native-filesystem-metadata.md](../reference/cli/native-filesystem-metadata.md)

## Context

The native adapter keeps a private, bounded ownership inventory under the operator's home (`.relavium-fs-private-v1/fs-staging-v1`). Allocating a reservation, cleaning up after a publication and a recovery pass each take an exclusive kernel claim on `inventory.lock`, **with one nonblocking attempt**: any failure, including "another process holds it", was returned as the `changed` refusal. The TypeScript host turns any native stage failure into a non-retryable `effect_needs_attention`.

Several processes on one home (two terminals, a terminal and an editor extension) are a supported way to work. The probes below ran on the built addon (macOS) with several writers **in one process, on separate descriptors**: those conflict under `flock` exactly as separate processes do, but a probe with two real OS processes is still owed. Two runs of the first probe agree: on 2026-11-17, 27 of 60 writes failed at stage and 3 ended uncertain; on 2026-11-18, 28 failed and 2 ended uncertain.

| Probe (before the change) | Result |
|---|---|
| Two concurrent writers, 30 writes each (2026-11-18) | 28 of 60 failed at stage with `changed:not_needed`; 2 more ended `possibly_published:uncertain` (the target was written, but the post-publication cleanup could not take the claim, so the outcome could not be settled); **30 of 60 completed cleanly** |
| 200 recovery passes while a lock-less churner created and removed `.reserved` entries (what a producer's pin does to its reservation) | 102 failed with `changed` (78 in the 2026-11-17 run); the same probe with no churn: 0 of 200 |

The second row is a separate defect: a scan lists an entry, then finds it gone, and calls the whole index changed. The TypeScript inventory reader, which every filesystem tool call runs, has the same two windows (the entry gone before it is opened, and unlinked after it was opened), so a harmless race could surface to the user as a refused call as well.

## Decision

1. **The allocation claim is waited for.** The nonblocking attempt is repeated about every two milliseconds while another handle holds the claim. Reserving and a recovery pass wait inside the operation's own two-second deadline and cancellation flag; a raised flag is `cancelled` and an exhausted deadline is the same `changed` refusal as before. **Cleanup after a publication and pre-pin cleanup wait under a fresh two-second budget and ignore cancellation, on purpose:** cleanup completion suppresses cancellation (ADR-0126), and a half-done cleanup is worse than a late one. Each claim site has its own budget, so one write that waits at the reservation, at a pre-pin cleanup and at a post-publication cleanup can wait several times two seconds in the worst case. Work done under a claim measures its budget from the moment the claim was **acquired**, not from before the wait, so a long wait cannot leave a cleanup half done. After the wait the lock file is opened by name again and must still be the file that was locked: another process may have unlinked and recreated it, and a claim on the orphaned inode would split the exclusion domain.
2. **At most all but one worker-pool thread waits at once.** Every native call runs on Node's shared worker pool (four threads unless `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE` says otherwise, which also serves `fs`, `dns` and `crypto`); parking all of it behind a stuck holder (a process stopped inside its critical section, a slow network home) would stall every other async call in the CLI, provider connections included. So at most `pool size - 1` threads per process wait (three by default). **A caller beyond that limit is not refused at once: it polls for a short grace of 100 ms**, so a fourth concurrent caller behind a holder that is merely busy (a holder in another process is the supported case) still gets through, while a stuck holder costs the one remaining thread only that grace per caller. Past the grace it gets the old `changed` refusal. Cancellation and the deadline are checked before the limit, so a cancelled caller is `cancelled`, never `changed`.
3. **Only contention is repeated.** `EWOULDBLOCK`, `EAGAIN` and `EINTR` for `flock`, `ERROR_LOCK_VIOLATION` for `LockFile`. A bad descriptor, a full lock table or any other error fails at once: retrying cannot fix it and must not be mistaken for a holder that will go away.
4. **The per-record claim stays a single attempt.** A held record claim means a live producer owns that record (recovery retains it). A writer holds its own record claim while it waits for the allocation claim, and recovery holds the allocation claim while it tries a record claim, so waiting on both would make a wait cycle.
5. **A scan skips an uncommitted entry that vanishes, in the Rust scan and in the TypeScript reader.** A producer pins without any claim a reader holds: it renames `.prepare` to `.record` and removes `.reserved`, and a completing write removes its `.record`. A scan that meets an uncommitted entry gone, either before it opens it or after (the unlinked file it holds has a link count of zero and its identity no longer matches), skips it; after a refusal the entry only counts as vanished when its name is really absent. In the TypeScript reader a committed `.record` that is unlinked after it was opened runs the whole scan again, as one that vanished before the open already did; in the Rust scan a `.record` and the lock file are removed only by holders of the allocation claim, which the scan also holds, so their absence stays a changed index. A file that is still there and fails a check stays fatal. A skipped entry **still counts toward the 128-nonce bound and is listed** (a recovery pass reports it as retained, which a mid-promotion entry is): a directory listing can omit an entry renamed while it runs, so leaving it out let `reserve` allocate past the bound, after which every scan, recovery included, failed closed. `reserve` still refuses at 128; the Rust scan tolerates eight more nonces so that the supported way out, an operator recovery pass, can still run. The TypeScript reader keeps its strict bound of 128: while 129 to 136 nonces are on disk, tool calls fail closed until recovery drains them (an availability gap, not a safety one).
6. **Two small hardenings in the same code.** `Record::encode` enforces every invariant `Record::decode` requires, through shared validators: a positive `i32` producer id, an owner and a locator within their bounds, a valid nonce, and an absolute locator **in normalized form** (written exactly as `Path::components` would write it back: no `.` or `..` component, no repeated or trailing separator) whose last name is `<prefix><pid>.<nonce>`. That is the rule the TypeScript reader applies as `resolve(stage) === stage`, so a record that can be written is accepted by both readers; the host passes realpath'd paths, so the rule is not reachable in practice, and an index from before this change needs no migration (the application is still in development). And a pin removes its `.reserved` only after re-proving that the file under the name is the one it created, as `cleanup` already does.

Considered: **a blocking lock call** with no polling (rejected: it cannot honour the deadline or the cancellation flag, it parks a worker thread without bound, and `flock`, `LockFileEx` and the Windows byte-range lock differ in what interrupts them); **retrying in the TypeScript host** on `changed:not_needed` (rejected: the host cannot tell contention from a real change, cannot retry a `possibly_published` cleanup because the producer has already handed the stage over, and a retry there touches the effect journal's semantics); **a distinct "busy" native result that the host retries without holding a thread** (not now: it is a host and effect-journal change, and the limit with its grace already keeps the pool free; it stays the option if the limit proves too tight); **serialising writers inside one process only** (rejected: the supported case is several processes); **a PID or lease file in front of the claim** (rejected: new stale-state failure modes where the kernel claim is already released on process death); **raising the nonblocking attempt count with no sleep** (rejected: it burns a core and still ends at an arbitrary count).

## Consequences

### Positive

- Concurrent writers queue instead of dropping. Measured on the rebuilt addon (macOS, in-process writers): two writers, 30 writes each, **60 of 60 completed cleanly (before: 30 of 60, plus 2 that wrote the target but ended uncertain)**; four writers 120 of 120; eight writers 240 of 240; two writers with a recovery loop running at the same time, 60 of 60 writes and 2,182 of 2,182 recoveries; the churn probe 200 of 200 (before: 98 of 200). No leftover inventory entries after any run. The real host write path (prepare, stage, publish, release through the built addon) is covered by a test that runs three concurrent writers and fails with `NativeWriteNeedsAttentionError` when the claim is one nonblocking attempt again (`fs-native-concurrency.test.ts`).
- Unrelated async work is not parked behind a stuck holder. With writers behind a claim held by another process, `fs.stat`, `dns.lookup` and `crypto.randomBytes` submitted 200 ms after the writers started took 0 ms with up to four writers, about 0.1 s with six and about 0.3 s with eight: each caller beyond the limit polls for its 100 ms grace, one after another, on the one thread that is never parked, so the delay is about 0.1 s per caller beyond the limit. The first version of the wait, with no limit and no grace, stalled them 1.8 s with four writers and 3.8 s with eight.
- A record can no longer be written that a later scan cannot read, and the TypeScript reader no longer refuses a tool call because a producer finished pinning at the wrong moment.

### Negative

- **Not every collision is absorbed.** Behind a holder that does not let go, three writers per process wait two seconds and every further one is refused after the 100 ms grace, so a fourth concurrent write in one process behind a busy holder in another process can still end as `changed`, which the host reports as `effect_needs_attention`, and a refused post-publication cleanup is `possibly_published:uncertain` with the target written. The host needs no change for the cases that now succeed, but it is not unaffected in the rest. Four concurrent native writes in one process need parallel workflow branches or several sessions; tool calls inside one session run one at a time.
- A waiting writer occupies a worker thread for up to two seconds, and the claim is not fair (`flock` and `LockFile` give no ordering). A recovery pass holds the claim for a whole scan, up to its own deadline, and can make a writer miss its own budget; the refusal is the existing one. A write can wait at several claim sites, so its worst case is a multiple of two seconds, and a cancelled write can spend up to two more in its cleanup.
- The evidence is in-process: a probe with two real OS processes (separate pools, separate waiter limits, process death) is still owed, and the supported-mode claim holds until it is run. The measurements ran on macOS; Linux and Windows are verified only by the eight-target CI.
- Windows is unproven here beyond a type check. A Windows scan can meet an entry in a delete-pending state (an access-denied or sharing-violation error instead of "not found"), which this ADR does not claim to handle; the stress test and the other POSIX-only tests are excluded from the Windows population for that reason.
- The wait widens the window in which another same-user process can replace the lock file; the identity check after the wait closes it for the claim just taken, and a same-user process could always do the rest.
- Outside this change, as separate items in the register: a cold-start race when two processes create the index hierarchy on a fresh home at the same moment (never probed), the Linux and Windows parity findings (a setgid parent directory, the link-count check on Windows, the Linux private-file parent check, the staged-payload re-proof), closing the producer's reservation handle after the pin removes it (a possible Windows hardening), and `recoverMetadataStaging`, which no product code calls yet.
